# Prior work on the observed median-versus-tail tradeoff

Reviewed 2026-10-03 after the completed V12 experiment. This follows the earlier [mechanism/remedy map](../../docs/runtime_io_prior_art_review.md) and addresses the specific findings in the [offline tradeoff audit](report.md). Primary sources were checked directly. Source findings below are distinguished from our inferences about their relevance. This is a targeted review, not an exhaustive proof of absence or novelty.

**The broad result already has a direct precedent: reducing Go execution parallelism can worsen p50 while improving tail latency under a CPU quota. Official Go policy discussions also explicitly anticipate latency costs from restricting short-term burst parallelism. Our phase-specific queue and post-work accounting provides a more detailed description of this one workload, but that difference does not establish an unanswered research question by itself.**

## Closest matches

|---|---|---|---|
| [Uber automaxprocs performance results](https://github.com/uber-go/automaxprocs#performance) | At a two-CPU quota, lowering GOMAXPROCS from 2 to 1 raises p50 from 0.84 to 1.46 ms while lowering p99.9 from 26.38 to 19.70 ms. ## Version-specific implications

The [Go 1.26.1 runtime documentation/source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/golang/go/go1.26.1/src/runtime/debug.go) specifies quota/period-aware defaults, upward rounding and a minimum of two unless logical CPU count or affinity is below two. Explicit GOMAXPROCS disables automatic updates. With this study's two-CPU affinity and half-CPU quota, the documented quota-aware calculation selects two; we nevertheless tested explicit settings, not automatic behavior.

The [automaxprocs v1.6.0 rounding implementation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/uber-go/automaxprocs/v1.6.0/internal/runtime/runtime.go) floors fractional quotas; its [configuration implementation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/uber-go/automaxprocs/v1.6.0/maxprocs/maxprocs.go) supplies a minimum of one and honors an explicit environment setting. This explains why a library policy and a runtime default can select different values. We installed neither library nor runtime change during V12. Setting one directly tests an already available remedy.

The historical [automaxprocs rounding discussion, PR #13](https://github.com/uber-go/automaxprocs/pull/13) also records the utilization-versus-throttling debate. Its arguments concern fractional budgets and supporting other processes; they are not measurements that establish our mechanism.

## Other sources screened against this observation

- [FireAxe, UCB/EECS-2024-220](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2024/EECS-2024-220.pdf), section 5.4, pp. 22–24: an allocation-heavy Go timer benchmark on a simulated four-core SoC has worse tail latency at GOMAXPROCS=1, attributed by the authors to serial execution with GC. Affinity is varied; a matching fractional quota is not established. This is a counterexample to a universal one-processor recommendation, not evidence that GC caused our median penalty.
- [Go issue #73964](https://github.com/golang/go/issues/73964): reports long scheduling gaps with very short sleeps and GOMAXPROCS=2, proposes runnext/local-queue explanations. It is a mechanism lead, not a confirmed match to the bursty quota/HTTP result.
- [Go issue #52934](https://github.com/golang/go/issues/52934): reports runnable-work starvation during continuous new goroutine creation, including GOMAXPROCS=1. It reinforces that the working setting here is not a universal fairness guarantee.
- The previously reviewed Shinjuku, Shenango, Caladan, interactive work-stealing and Autothrottle papers remain background for preemption, allocation and admission. Their systems/resource contracts differ substantially; no newly retrieved evidence makes them direct explanations of this exact p50/p99 contrast.

## What the comparison permits us to say

| Observation in our audit | Status after comparison |
|---|---|
| Lower parallelism improves the tail under a tight quota | Established direction with prior evidence and existing remedies. Locally reproduced here. |
| Lower parallelism can worsen typical latency | Directly precedented by Uber's p50/p99.9 data and anticipated in Go's burst-policy rationale. This is not a newly discovered tradeoff. |
| The cost is concentrated in later high-phase arrivals; recovery improves in low phases | A detailed local characterization consistent with backlog and short-term parallelism effects. The reviewed sources do not quantify this exact schedule, but a different schedule alone does not establish novelty. |
| Queue and completion-to-handler-resume waiting increase near the median while long entry waits disappear | Verified stage accounting in this workload. Exact scheduling/quota causes remain unassigned. Generic scheduling and queueing precedents exist. |
| CPU/job falls and service outcomes improve | Capacity/efficiency and scheduling change together; a fairness-only interpretation is unsupported. The review cannot apportion their contributions. |
| A future mechanism or remedy claim | Requires evidence of an unresolved limitation and checking the closest work against that specific claim. No such claim is made by this review. |

An evidence-based working explanation is that less simultaneous execution changes both the distribution of CPU opportunities and effective processing cost. During high phases this produces more ordinary application waiting while suppressing rare, much longer entry delays. This is **an inference consistent with the data and existing explanations**, not a measured scheduler pathway.

The next decision can use that distinction: first determine whether an additional mechanism question matters for a specified service objective, rather than assuming that a tradeoff automatically creates novelty. This review deliberately does not start another trial or select a new intervention.

## Search and coverage record

Queries covered GOMAXPROCS/CPU quota with p50/p99 tradeoffs, bursty HTTP scheduling and queue delay, thread-management papers with queue accounting, and citation-following from Go proposal #73193 to the rounding discussion. Close claims above were checked in original repositories, official documentation or primary papers. General blogs, derivative copies of Uber's table and unsupported blanket recommendations were excluded as supporting evidence. Bibliographic dates use source metadata rather than search-engine crawl dates.

The review preserves and narrows the earlier map instead of treating every scheduling paper as a close match. It is limited by the workload detail reported in those sources, by incomplete retrieval of some issue discussion comments, and by the absence of an exhaustive citation search. It cannot establish that no other researcher has analyzed the exact combination of version, fractional quota, burst phases and outcome accounting.
